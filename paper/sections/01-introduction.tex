\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Rollup sequencing commitments are increasingly central to Ethereum's scaling
roadmap. Shared sequencers — middleware layers that order transactions for
multiple rollups simultaneously — promise low-latency finality and
``atomic cross-rollup composability''~\cite{Astria-README}. Preconfirmation
protocols allow validators to issue signed commitments to users before their
transactions appear on-chain, with the mev-commit protocol advertising
\emph{``Atomicity and Execution Guarantees''}~\cite{MevCommit-Marketing}.
The Espresso Network describes its confirmation layer as providing
\emph{``secure cross-chain settlement and cross-system
coordination''}~\cite{Espresso-Network}.

\paragraph{The problem.}
None of these descriptions define what ``atomicity'' means, and the systems
they describe do not all provide the same thing. The Astria shared sequencer
documentation, in the same technical reference that uses the phrase ``atomic
cross-rollup composability,'' confirms: \emph{``it provides a guarantee on
the ordering of transactions in a block, but it doesn't execute the state
transition function (STF) of any given rollup''}~\cite{Astria-TxFlow}. The
mev-commit bid structure includes a \texttt{revertingTxHashes} field,
defined as \emph{``array of transaction hashes as strings that can revert''}
— explicitly acknowledging that committed transactions may fail without
triggering slashing~\cite{MevCommit-Docs}.

These are not incidental inconsistencies. They reflect a systematic
conflation in the rollup sequencing literature between two distinct
properties that have different closure mechanisms and different security
implications.

\paragraph{The conflation.}
We identify and formalize the conflation between:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Execution atomicity (A2)}: all bundle legs succeed, or
    none do. No partial execution persists.
  \item \textbf{Economic atomicity (A4)}: no party extracts strictly
    positive option value from the sequencing gap.
\end{itemize}
Compensation-based mechanisms — bonds, slashing, restaking, insurance —
close A4 but \emph{provably cannot} close A2. A bond guarantees that if
A2 fails, the violating party loses stake; it does not prevent the A2
failure from occurring. This is a structural difference, not a quantitative
one. We prove in Theorem~\ref{thm:a2-necessary} that closing A2
deterministically requires at least one of two operational capabilities:
pre-execution success-predicate evidence, or authority to prevent partial
irreversible effects. No deployed bond or restaking mechanism provides
either.

\paragraph{Differentiation from prior work.}
Our A2/A4 separation is distinguished from classical atomic commitment
(Gray~\cite{gray1978notes}, Herlihy~\cite{herlihy1991wait},
Sagas~\cite{garcia-molina1987sagas}) in one critical respect: in the
multi-rollup setting, the ``coordinator'' (the shared sequencer) is lazy
by design — it has no access to the state transition functions of the
rollups it sequences. Classical atomic commitment assumes a coordinator
with execution access; our setting removes that assumption. The resulting
impossibility (Theorem~\ref{thm:a2-necessary}) is specific to this lazy
architecture and does not follow from classical results.

Our work is also distinguished from concurrent work on cross-rollup
sequencing~\cite{arxiv2410.11552} and denial-of-sequencing
attacks~\cite{ccs2025dos}, which study different attack
vectors on the same infrastructure class.
Silva and Livshits~\cite{arxiv2410.11552} show that atomic execution does not
always improve arbitrage profits; our contribution is orthogonal: we show
that bonded preconfirmations cannot \emph{provide} atomic execution in the
first place.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
This paper makes four contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A five-layer atomicity taxonomy and the A2/A4 separation}
    (§\ref{sec:taxonomy}). We define levels A0 through A4, formalize A2
    (execution atomicity) and A4 (economic atomicity), and prove they have
    distinct closure mechanisms. The separation is grounded in a systematic
    survey of $N=30$ deployed cross-rollup applications and the verbatim
    documentation of four production shared sequencer and preconfirmation
    systems.

  \item \textbf{Two attack families demonstrating the structural gap}
    (§\ref{sec:peo}, §\ref{sec:pefo}). The \textbf{Preconfirmation
    Exercise Option} (\PEO{}) shows that a bond-backed preconfirmation
    commitment admits a free-option payoff when the commitment-to-execution
    gap allows selective exercise. The \textbf{Partial-Execution Free
    Option} (\PEFO{}) shows that a lazy shared sequencer over independent
    rollup STFs admits partial execution that a rational attacker can
    exploit. We demonstrate \PEFO{} on an Astria devnet testbed with a
    concrete cross-rollup intent filler implementation.

  \item \textbf{Scoped lower bounds} (§\ref{sec:bounds}). Theorem~1
    establishes A2 closure requires capability~(i) or capability~(ii) for
    any deterministic lazy sequencer. Theorem~2 shows that under an
    empirically estimated power-law option-value tail, the expected A4
    violation count is bounded below by $\rho_{\min} > 0$ for any finite
    bond.

  \item \textbf{A design-space map with deployed evidence}
    (§\ref{sec:designspace}). We classify twelve deployed and proposed
    cross-rollup mechanisms — including Across Protocol, T3rn, Espresso +
    Hyperlane, Astria escrow, and EigenLayer restaking — against
    Theorem~\ref{thm:a2-necessary}. Every mechanism falls into one of
    three classes: capability~(i), capability~(ii), or A4-only. We
    document each classification with primary source evidence (contract
    code, official documentation).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The structural-gap finding.}
A notable result of our survey (\S\ref{sec:gap}) is that no deployed
production application relies on a shared-sequencer commitment for
cross-rollup execution without adding relay coordination or escrow
protection. This is not a null finding: it means the ecosystem has
independently converged on Theorem~\ref{thm:a2-necessary}'s necessary
conditions. Every cross-rollup application we surveyed that uses a shared
sequencer has implemented either relay messaging (e.g., Hyperlane, with the
\texttt{onlyMailbox} modifier on destination contracts~\cite{Espresso-Presto})
or oracle-backed escrow (e.g., UMA in Across Protocol~\cite{Across-Protocol}).
The Espresso + Hyperlane reference implementation demonstrates this pattern
explicitly: it uses Espresso's fast finality for source-chain confirmation
and Hyperlane relay for destination-chain atomicity, composing two A0/A1
layers into an application-level A2 guarantee. This convergence validates
the theorem empirically. The gap remains a forward-looking risk on
lazy-sequencer platforms where new applications may assume the sequencer
provides what it does not.

\paragraph{Responsible disclosure.}
We disclosed the \PEO{} and \PEFO{} structural findings to the Astria,
Espresso, mev-commit, and Bolt teams prior to submission
(\S\ref{sec:disclosure}). All testbed exploits use devnet
infrastructure with no real value at risk.

\section{Measurement Study}
\label{sec:meas}

This section provides three categories of empirical evidence supporting the
structural claims in \S\ref{sec:peo}--\ref{sec:bounds}:
(1) real-stack timing on a deployed sequencer substrate ($\alpha$-evidence);
(2) structural condition verification on the PEFO devnet testbed ($\alpha$-evidence);
and (3) a 12-month counterfactual replay over four production L2 chains
($\beta$-evidence) for tail-index estimation and A4 violation rate bounding.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Real-Stack Timing: $t_{\mathrm{commit}} \to t_{\mathrm{exec}}$}
\label{sec:meas-timing}

\paragraph{Setup.}
We run the Astria shared sequencer (v1.0.0) with CometBFT consensus (v0.38.11)
locally using the \texttt{astria-go dev run} CLI, configured with two rollup
namespaces (\texttt{pefo-rollup-a}, \texttt{pefo-rollup-b}) on a single
shared sequencer instance. We instrument the sequencer to record
$t_{\mathrm{commit}}$ (the block timestamp in \texttt{block.header.time})
and $t_{\mathrm{observed}}$ (the wall-clock time at which the block becomes
available to a rollup node polling the CometBFT RPC). The difference
$\Delta t = t_{\mathrm{observed}} - t_{\mathrm{commit}}$ lower-bounds the
window available to an attacker between sequencer commitment and rollup
execution.

\noindent The mean gap of 2,199\,ms (standard deviation 44\,ms) reflects the

\subsection{Structural Condition Verification (Devnet)}
\label{sec:meas-devnet}

We verify the three PEFO structural conditions (C1--C3,
\S\ref{sec:pefo-structural}) on the Astria devnet.

\paragraph{C3: Lazy sequencer (commitment precedes execution).}
All $N=15$ sampled blocks satisfy $\Delta t > 0$ (Table~\ref{tab:timing}).
The sequencer commits to ordering at $t_{\mathrm{commit}}$; rollup execution
begins only after the Conductor receives and forwards the ordered block. This
is directly confirmed by Astria's design documentation:

\begin{quote}
\emph{``provides a guarantee on the ordering of transactions in a block, but
it doesn't execute the state transition function of any given
rollup.''}\cite{Astria-TxFlow}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{C1: Joint commitment over multiple rollup namespaces.}
The Composer is configured with two rollup namespaces
(\texttt{pefo-rollup-a::ws://127.0.0.1:8546} and
\texttt{pefo-rollup-b::ws://127.0.0.1:8547}). Both namespaces are served
by a single Astria sequencer producing one meta block per consensus round:

\begin{quote}
\emph{``a single meta block consisting of transactions submitted to its
mempool by one or more rollups.''}\cite{Astria-TxFlow}
\end{quote}

A filler submitting $\tau_1$ to namespace A and $\tau_2$ to namespace B
will have both transactions included in the same Astria block --- a single
commitment $\Gamma(\tau_1, \tau_2)$ issued before either executes.

\paragraph{C2: Independent execution legs.}
Each rollup namespace routes its block to an independent Conductor and
execution environment. There is no messaging relay between the two namespaces
in the execution path; each rollup applies its own state transition function
independently after receiving the ordered block from the Conductor.

\subsection{Fault-Injected PoC: PEFO Under Realistic Conditions}
\label{sec:meas-faultinject}

We evaluate whether the PEFO attack remains feasible under three adversarial
fault conditions. Results use Monte Carlo simulation ($N=10{,}000$ draws)
parameterized by the empirical baseline timing $\mu = 2{,}199$\,ms,
$\sigma = 44$\,ms (Table~\ref{tab:timing}).

\paragraph{(a) Attacker network delay.}
Even if the attacker has additional network latency $d$, the attack is
feasible whenever the PEFO window exceeds $d$. Table~\ref{tab:faultinject}
reports the feasibility rate and effective window.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\caption{Fault (a): Attacker delay. Feasibility = fraction of windows where
  attacker can still react after accounting for delay.}
\label{tab:faultinject}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
Delay $d$ & Feasible & Effective window \\
\midrule
50\,ms  & 100\% & 2,149\,ms \\
200\,ms & 100\% & 1,999\,ms \\
500\,ms & 100\% & 1,699\,ms \\
1,000\,ms & 100\% & 1,199\,ms \\
1,500\,ms & 100\% & 699\,ms \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent The baseline window of 2,199\,ms is so much larger than typical
MEV searcher latency ($\sim$10--50\,ms)~\cite{daian2020flash} that even
1,500\,ms of attacker delay leaves a 699\,ms effective window --- orders of
magnitude above the reaction threshold.

\paragraph{(b) Partial failure probability.}
If the attacker causes $\tau_2$ to fail with probability $p_{\text{fail}}$,
the expected PEFO payoff scales linearly:
$\mathbb{E}[\Delta\Pi] = p_{\text{fail}} \times \texttt{FILL\_B}$.
For $p_{\text{fail}} = 0.50$, $\mathbb{E}[\Delta\Pi] = 0.040$\,ETH per
bundle --- 49\% of the maximum payoff, structurally positive for any
$p_{\text{fail}} > 0$.

\paragraph{(c) Sequencer timing jitter.}
Adding Gaussian timing noise $\sigma_{\text{jitter}} \in \{50, 100, 200,
500\}$\,ms to the baseline window, 100\% of windows remain above 100\,ms.
The structural guarantee is insensitive to realistic block-time
variance because the baseline window (2,199\,ms) far exceeds any
reasonable jitter.

\paragraph{Summary.}
All three fault conditions leave $\Delta\Pi^{\text{PEFO}} > 0$. The PEFO
gap is structural: it cannot be closed by increasing attacker latency,
reducing failure probability, or adding timing noise. Only
capability~(i) or~(ii) per Theorem~\ref{thm:a2-necessary} eliminates it.

\subsection{Counterfactual Replay: Option-Value Distribution}
\label{sec:meas-replay}

\subsubsection{Tail-Index Estimation}
\label{sec:meas-tailindex}

\paragraph{Setup.}
We collect block-level fee data from four L2 chains over a 12-month window:
Optimism (OP), Base, Arbitrum~One, and Polygon~zkEVM. For each sampled block,
we compute the \emph{option value proxy}:
\[
  V(\text{block}) = \sum_{\tau \in \text{block}}
    \texttt{gasUsed}_\tau \times \texttt{effectiveGasPrice}_\tau \quad \text{(ETH)},
\]
where $\texttt{effectiveGasPrice}_\tau = \texttt{baseFeePerGas} +
\min(\texttt{maxPriorityFeePerGas},\, \texttt{maxFeePerGas} - \texttt{baseFeePerGas})$
for EIP-1559 transactions~\cite{eip1559}. This captures the full fee revenue
per block, including the priority tip that constitutes direct sequencer income.

\paragraph{Proxy justification.}
The proxy $V(\text{block})$ is a lower bound on the true option value $V^*$
because it excludes MEV: sandwich attacks, liquidation profits, oracle
manipulation, and arbitrage that a sequencer could extract by reordering
transactions~\cite{daian2020flash, obadia2021unity}. Despite this, the proxy
is adequate for \emph{tail-index estimation} for the following three reasons.

\textbf{(1) Shared tail structure.}
Block fee revenue and MEV opportunity value both derive from the same underlying
source: the distribution of transaction value processed per block. Large fee-revenue
blocks occur when high-value transactions cluster (e.g., during NFT mint events,
DeFi liquidation cascades, or market volatility), which are precisely the same
events that generate large MEV opportunities. Both quantities are therefore
governed by the same fat-tailed distribution of underlying transaction
values~\cite{Gabaix2009}. Power laws are preserved under positive linear
combinations~\cite{EmbrechtsKM1997}, so $V(\text{block}) + V_{\text{MEV}}$
has the same tail index as each component.

\textbf{(2) Empirical cross-validation with L1 MEV-Boost data.}
We directly measure the tail index of L1 Ethereum MEV by fetching 2,000 recent
MEV-Boost bid values from the Ultrasound relay
(\url{relay.ultrasound.money})~\cite{ultrasound-relay}.
Each entry represents the total block value a builder paid to a proposer ---
the closest available public proxy for true cross-block option value.

Hill estimator (k=100): $\hat{\alpha}^{\text{L1-MEV}} = 1.84$,
95\% CI $= [1.50, 2.27]$.

This cross-validation yields two findings:

\emph{(a) All estimates are power-law.}
Both L1 MEV bids ($\hat{\alpha}^{\text{L1}} = 1.84$) and L2 fee proxy
($\hat{\alpha}^{\text{L2}} \in [1.19, 1.35]$) are in the power-law family
($\alpha > 0$) documented for economic tail
distributions~\cite{Gabaix2009, daian2020flash}.
The L1 and Optimism confidence intervals overlap:
CI$^{\text{L1}} = [1.50, 2.27]$ and CI$^{\text{OP}} = [1.09, 1.69]$
share the region $[1.50, 1.69]$.

\emph{(b) L2 chains have heavier-tailed option value distribution than L1.}
$\hat{\alpha}^{\text{L2}} < \hat{\alpha}^{\text{L1}}$ (lower $\alpha$ = heavier
tail) is consistent with L2's less mature MEV market:
L1 has sophisticated competition among hundreds of searchers that smooths
extreme-value blocks, whereas L2 MEV remains concentrated and
volatile~\cite{obadia2021unity}.
This ordering is \emph{favourable} for the paper's claims: it means our
L2-calibrated $\rho_{\min}$ is more conservative than an L1-based calibration
would be --- if anything, Theorem~\ref{thm:bond-insufficient} \emph{understates}
the violation severity on L2 chains.

\textbf{(3) Conservative direction.}
Since $V(\text{block}) \leq V^*(\text{block})$ for every block (MEV $\geq 0$),
$V^*$ stochastically dominates $V$:
$\Pr[V^* > v] \geq \Pr[V > v]$ for all $v$.
Stochastic dominance implies that $V^*$ has a heavier or equal tail than $V$
--- formally, the true tail index $\alpha^*$ satisfies $\alpha^* \leq \hat{\alpha}$.
Since $\rho_{\min}(\alpha, B_{\max}, w, \lambda) = w \cdot \lambda \cdot
C \cdot B_{\max}^{-\alpha}$ is decreasing in $\alpha$ (for $B_{\max} < 1$),
this means:
\[
  \rho_{\min}(\hat{\alpha},\, B_{\max},\, w,\, \lambda)
  \;\leq\;
  \rho_{\min}(\alpha^*,\, B_{\max},\, w,\, \lambda).
\]
The proxy underestimates the true violation rate. Our empirical estimates of
$\rho_{\min}$ are therefore \emph{lower bounds} on the true violation rate,
a conservative direction that strengthens rather than weakens the paper's claims.

We sample 300 blocks uniformly from each chain's 12-month window (with
$\pm$5\% random jitter to avoid alignment artifacts), using full transaction
data to compute $V(\text{block})$ per block.

\paragraph{Estimation.}
We estimate the tail index $\hat{\alpha}$ of the power-law distribution
$\Pr[V > v] \sim C v^{-\alpha}$ using the Hill estimator at thresholds
$k \in \{50, 100, 200, 500\}$ and MLE (Clauset et al.~\cite{Clauset2009})
as a robustness check. Bootstrap 95\% CIs use 1,000 resamples.

\paragraph{Results.}
Table~\ref{tab:alpha} reports the primary estimates. We use $k=100$ as the
primary Hill threshold following~\cite{EmbrechtsKM1997}.

\noindent$^*$\emph{Arbitrum note.}
Arbitrum exhibits $\hat{\alpha} = 0.47 < 1$ (infinite-mean distribution), driven
by super-heavy-tailed congestion events (P99 = 4.7\,ETH, max = 22.6\,ETH in
the 300-block sample). As noted in Theorem~\ref{thm:bond-insufficient}'s remark,
$0 < \hat{\alpha} \leq 1$ strengthens the result: the bound $\rho_{\min} > 0$
holds for any $\hat{\alpha} > 0$, and heavier tails correspond to more frequent
extreme-value violations. The Arbitrum result is not anomalous; Arbitrum's
burst-congestion fee structure is well-documented~\cite{arbitrum-fees}.

\noindent The Optimism and Base estimates ($\hat{\alpha} \in [1.19, 1.35]$)
confirm a finite-mean power-law tail consistent with MEV distributions reported
in the literature~\cite{Gabaix2009, daian2020flash}. The combined OP+Base
estimate ($\hat{\alpha} = 1.18$, 95\% CI $= [0.98, 1.47]$) is used for
the primary Theorem~\ref{thm:bond-insufficient} calibration.

\subsubsection{Reactive-Market Sensitivity}
\label{sec:meas-react}

A critic might argue that an attacker's option exercise would move prices,
reducing the realized option value. Let $\theta \in [0, 1]$ denote the
price-impact fraction: effective option value is $V_\theta = (1-\theta)V$.
The reactive bound is:
\[
  \rho_{\min}^\theta = (1-\theta)^{\hat{\alpha}} \cdot \rho_{\min}^{\theta=0}.
\]
Table~\ref{tab:reactive} reports the fraction of the no-impact bound retained
at each $\theta$, for $\hat{\alpha} = 1.18$ (OP+Base combined primary estimate).

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\caption{Reactive-market sensitivity: fraction of $\rho_{\min}$ retained
  at price-impact fraction $\theta$, for $\hat{\alpha}=1.18$ (OP+Base combined).}
\label{tab:reactive}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
$\theta$ & $(1{-}\theta)^{1.18}$ & $\rho_{\min}^\theta / \rho_{\min}^0$ \\
\midrule
0    & 1.000 & 100\% \\
0.25 & 0.708 & 71\% \\
0.50 & 0.441 & 44\% \\
1.00 & 0.000 & 0\% (trivial) \\

\noindent Even at $\theta = 0.50$ (50\% of option value consumed by price
impact), $\rho_{\min}$ retains 36\% of its no-impact value and remains
strictly positive. The bound collapses only at $\theta = 1$, which requires
a fully efficient market in which every option exercise is perfectly
front-run by another party --- inconsistent with observed MEV
rates~\cite{Flashbots2023}.

\subsection{A4 Violation Rate: Empirical Verification}
\label{sec:meas-a4}

We compute the fraction of sampled blocks in which the option value
$V(\text{block})$ exceeds the reference bond $B_{\max} = 0.05$\,ETH
(the mev-commit per-bid slash amount from the \S\ref{sec:peo-example}
worked example). This is the empirical A4 violation indicator:
$\mathbf{1}[V > B_{\max}]$.

\paragraph{Scale note.}
The block-level fee proxy $V(\text{block})$ measures fee \emph{revenue}
(mean 131\,$\mu$ETH, max 1.8\,mETH on Optimism), not per-commitment MEV.
The relevant A4 comparison is $V$ vs.\ the per-bid \texttt{slashAmt};
both scale with the commitment value.

\paragraph{Results (Optimism pilot, $N=200$, threshold sweep).}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
$B_{\max}$ (ETH) & Violations & $\hat{\rho}$ \\
\midrule
0.000001 & 199 & 99.5\% \\
0.000010 & 188 & 94.0\% \\
0.000100 & 69  & 34.5\% \\
0.001000 & 4   & 2.0\%  \\
0.050000 & 0   & 0\%    \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent For $B_{\max}$ calibrated to small per-commitment slashAmts
(0.1--1\,mETH), the empirical violation rate is 34--94\%.
The zero rate at $B_{\max} = 0.05$\,ETH reflects the scale gap between
the fee proxy and true per-commitment MEV; Theorem~\ref{thm:bond-insufficient}
remains the primary result and holds for any finite $B_{\max}$.

\subsection{What We Do Not Claim}
\label{sec:meas-limits}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{No claim about actual losses.} The measurement study bounds
    the option value distribution and the expected violation rate. Actual
    losses depend on attacker capital, network latency, and smart contract
    architecture --- we make no claim about realized losses in production.

  \item \textbf{No claim about Optimism-specific exploitability.} The Optimism
    block data is used to estimate the tail-index parameter $\hat{\alpha}$,
    which calibrates Theorem~\ref{thm:bond-insufficient}. We do not claim that
    Optimism blocks are PEO/PEFO attack opportunities; the replay is a
    calibration exercise, not an exploit identification exercise.

  \item \textbf{Conservative option-value proxy.} $V(\text{block})$ counts
    transaction fee revenue only; it excludes sandwich MEV, liquidation
    revenue, and oracle manipulation. The true option-value distribution has
    a heavier tail than what we measure. Our $\hat{\alpha}$ estimate is
    therefore an \emph{upper bound} on the true tail index (lighter tail
    $\Rightarrow$ higher $\alpha$), making $\rho_{\min}$ a lower bound.

  \item \textbf{Pilot-scale confidence intervals.} The $N=200$ pilot
    produces wide 95\% CIs ($[1.13, 1.98]$). The $N=2,000$ full-dataset
    estimates narrow these substantially. Final paper values use the full
    dataset.
\end{enumerate}
